# Meta campaign structure

One practitioner's operating structure (Nick Theriot, agency account of about 50 clients, reported by him, not independently checked). The point of it: **structure is about 10% of the work and should stay boring; creative is the other 90%.** Do not invent a cleverer structure. Spend the effort on `ad-angle-testing`.

**House rules win.** Read `ads/house-rules.md` first if it exists. Spend, launching and budget changes are drafts and proposals until the owner approves. Where a format below breaks a business rule, drop the format, not the rule.

## The structure

| Level | Rule |
|---|---|
| Campaign | One per product, one country per campaign. Campaign budget optimization (budget set at campaign level; Meta splits it). |
| Ad set | One per **idea** (one angle or message). No audience stacking. |
| Ads in the ad set | Three versions of the same video that differ only in the opening (he says the first three seconds; treat the exact length as a test variable where the business says so). One primary text, one headline. |
| Bidding | Plain lowest cost. He reports this beat the bid caps and cost caps clients asked for. |

## How this fits with `ad-angle-testing`

An ad set holds **one angle in one format**; its three ads are three openings. Testing one angle in three formats means three ad sets. For an unproven message, launch the cheapest format first and add the others once it spends. Finished videos that already exist go in as they are; re-cutting an opening is not new production. Three openings is the launch number; five openings is for an angle that has already proven itself.

## Small budgets

The source gives no minimum. A small budget supports few ideas: prefer fewer ad sets over thin ones, say how thin the read will be, and expect Meta to give most of the budget to one ad set. If there are too few purchases for Meta to learn from, optimize for the nearest earlier event that has volume and say that you did.

## Changing things

- **Budget:** move 20% up or down at a time. No bigger jumps. The source gives no trigger or waiting time for a raise: propose one, label it as your assumption, and get it approved before launch.
- **Pausing an ad is not cutting budget.** A stop rule written before launch applies to one ad or one idea. The campaign budget stays.
- **Cost per result too high:** the first answer is harder creative testing (new ideas, new openings), not cutting budget.
- **A winner:** duplicate it into a new campaign for another country (he cites UK and Canada after the US) before inventing something new.
- **New buyer types:** pull them from customer reviews and real customer language, then give each its own idea and ad set.

## Which format to build in

Test a new message in the cheapest format first. Rebuild it in a costly format only after the cheap version has taken real spend.

| Step | Format | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Native: a long-story, organic-looking photo post | Cheapest |
| 2 | Animated dialogue, generated presenter video, or story-led talking video | Higher; only for proven messages |

His highest spenders were long-story native photo ads, animated dialogue, generated presenter videos and "story while doing something else" videos. Check each against the business's rules on presenters, invented stories and testimonials before using it.

## Keep a record of losers

Keep the winning AND losing native ads with their numbers. Writers and models learn from the contrast, not from winners alone.

## Output when asked for a structure

A table: campaign (product, country, daily budget) -> ad sets (one idea each, named by the idea) -> ads (which three openings). Then the budget rule, what triggers a review, and what needs the owner's approval. Name anything you assumed.

## Common mistakes

- One ad set holding several unrelated ideas: the result cannot be read.
- Several countries in one campaign.
- Answering a bad week by cutting budget or adding bid caps instead of testing new creative.
- Building an expensive animated version of a message that has never spent.
